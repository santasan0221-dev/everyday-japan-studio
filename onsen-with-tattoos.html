Onsen With Tattoos: House Rule, Not Law

Checked against official Japanese sources · Updated November 2026

"Can I use a Japanese hot spring if I have tattoos?" The honest answer is: it depends on the bath. That sounds like a dodge, but it is the actual structure of the situation — and once you see the structure, you can find out any facility's answer before you travel. No guide can promise you entry to a specific bath. What you can have is a method.

Layer one: the law says nothing about tattoos

The Public Bath Act — the national law for public bathhouses, dating from 1948 — orders operators to refuse exactly one category of person: someone recognised as having a contagious disease. Tattoos are not mentioned anywhere in the Act. There is no Japanese law that bans tattoos in baths. Every tattoo rule you will meet is a house rule, set by the individual facility in its terms of use.

Layer two: the government has pushed the other way

On 16 March 2016, the Japan Tourism Agency formally wrote to the national hotel, ryokan and onsen associations saying that refusing a bather solely because they have a tattoo is "not appropriate" — pointing out that tattoos are a matter of religion, culture and fashion for many people, and that a tattoo is not a hygiene problem. The notice suggested three model responses: ask the guest to cover the tattoo with a seal (small tattoos, about palm-size, need no measure), invite them at quieter hours, or offer a private bath.

But read the notice for what it is: a request, not an order. It does not force any bath to let you in. You are asking within the facility's discretion, not asserting a right — which is also why asking politely and early works better than arguing at the desk.

Layer three: what facilities actually do

Any given bath will give you one of three answers:

  • Yes — tattoos accepted as they are.
  • Yes, with conditions — cover the tattoo with a seal, keep it small, come at a quieter time, or use the private bath instead.
  • No — tattooed guests not accepted, covered or not.

The most recent official nationwide count is the Tourism Agency's 2015 survey — and it needs handling with care. Of 3,768 hotels and inns surveyed, only 581 replied. Among those replies, 55.9% refused tattooed bathers, 30.6% accepted them, and 12.9% accepted them with conditions. That is a dated baseline from a low-response survey, not today's odds for any specific bath. There has been no newer official nationwide survey, and anyone quoting you a precise current percentage — in either direction — is guessing.

One town where the answer is simply yes

Kinosaki Onsen in Hyogo is the model case of a town that decided. The official tourism site of Toyooka City, Visit Kinosaki, states in its FAQ that people with decorative tattoos are allowed into all seven of Kinosaki's public bathhouses. Read it precisely, though: the seven public baths (sotoyu) say yes — your ryokan's own in-house bath is a separate question with its own rule. Two practical caveats from the same official FAQ, checked in November 2026: one of the seven, Satono-Yu, has been closed for renovation since April 2024, and each bathhouse has a weekly closing day — so "seven baths" rarely means seven open baths on a given day. A one-day pass to the public baths costs ¥1,500 for adults; guests of town inns usually receive one at check-in.

How to check any bath before you go

  1. Read the official site. Look for a FAQ, a bathing-guide page, or the terms of use. If the site states a policy, that is your answer — follow it exactly, including conditions. Learn three words: 入れ墨 and 刺青 both mean tattoo, and お断り signals a refusal.
  2. If the site is silent, ask. Silence means nothing either way. Email the facility — or ask through your booking platform — about three things specifically: tattoos as-is, tattoos covered with a seal, and a private bath if the shared bath is a no.
  3. If the shared bath is a no, switch bath type, not town. A reservable private or family bath at the same facility, then a public bathhouse or day spa in the same town — in onsen towns these are different operations with different rules. A no at your inn says nothing about the town's public baths.
  4. Consider the workarounds the government itself suggested: cover seals (sold at drugstores, Don Quijote, online, and sometimes at the front desk), quieter hours, private baths, or a room with its own bath.
  5. Re-check shortly before you go. Policies change with ownership and renovations. A two-minute re-check of the official page in the week of your trip is cheap insurance.

One more distinction that trips people up: a "yes" for staying guests is not automatically a "yes" for day visitors, and the reverse also happens. Ask about the exact bath, in the exact way you will use it.

And whatever the tattoo answer: the manners rules apply to everyone. Wash your whole body before entering the bath, keep your towel out of the water, bathe naked, keep hair out of the water. None of that is about tattoos — it is about sharing water.
