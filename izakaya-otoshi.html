Otoshi: The Dish You Didn't Order at the Izakaya

Checked against official Japanese sources · Updated November 2026

Minutes after you sit down at an izakaya — Japan's pub — a small dish arrives at your table: simmered vegetables, a little sashimi, potato salad, pickles. You did not order it. You cannot pick it. And when the bill comes, it is on there. First-timers tend to land on one of two theories: it's a free sample, or it's a scam. It is neither. It is the otoshi.

What it is: the seat charge, served as food

The otoshi is the izakaya's per-person seat charge, delivered as a bite-sized appetiser instead of as a line item. GO TOKYO, the Tokyo metropolitan tourism guide, puts the charge at usually ¥200–¥1,000 per person. The Consumer Hotline for Tourists, run by the National Consumer Affairs Center of Japan, explains it the same way to visitors: the otoshi is part of izakaya culture and functions as a table charge, paid at the end of the evening.

Why food instead of a fee? Because of how an izakaya evening starts. Drinks are ordered first — the traditional opening is toriaezu bīru, "beer, for now" — and food takes time to prepare. The otoshi is something to nibble while you wait for the first real dishes. It arrives automatically, before you have ordered anything, for every person at the table.

Can you refuse it?

Sometimes — and that is the honest answer. Practices differ by shop. Some places will let you decline if you say so the moment you sit down, before the dish is prepared. Many will not: GO TOKYO describes the otoshi as coming automatically rather than being ordered. The practical approach is to treat it as the price of the seat. It is a shop's charge, set by the shop — not a law, and not negotiable in the way a law never is — but building your evening on refusing it will disappoint you more often than it saves you money.

One situation is different: allergies. The otoshi often contains fish or meat, and you did not choose it. Tell staff about dietary restrictions early — before the dish lands, if you can. That conversation is always legitimate, charge or no charge.

The other charge to ask about

Some shops add a separate seating charge (sekiryō) on top of the otoshi. The Consumer Hotline for Tourists flags this explicitly, because it surprises visitors twice in one evening. If charges matter to you, ask before you order: "Sekiryō wa kakarimasu ka?" — "Is there a seat charge?" Asking is normal; nobody will think less of you.

Your izakaya bill, when it comes, is built from what you ordered, plus the otoshi per person, plus any separate seat charge, and at some places a late-night surcharge for guests arriving late. Menu prices in Japan are displayed tax-included — everything eaten and drunk at the table carries 10% consumption tax — so there is no extra tax line to add, and no tip on top: tipping is not a custom in Japan, and cash left behind creates confusion, not gratitude. If a charge on the bill genuinely makes no sense, ask at the register what it is ("Kore wa nan no ryōkin desu ka?"). If a dispute truly cannot be resolved at the shop, the Consumer Hotline for Tourists advises visitors on exactly this kind of problem, in English and other languages: 03-5449-0906, weekdays.

While you're at the table

  • The wet towel (oshibori) is for your hands — hands only, per JNTO's etiquette guidance. Using it on your face is the classic tourist tell. In winter it arrives warm; that part is just hospitality.
  • The first round is a custom, not a rule. "Beer, for now" exists because beer pours fast while everyone studies the menu. Any drink — a highball, tea, a soft drink — can open the evening. What matters is that everyone's first drink arrives together, so the table can toast: kanpai. Nobody starts before the toast.
  • Order food in rounds. Two or three small plates at a time, shared by the table, more as the table empties. Nobody expects one big order.
  • The closer has a name. Near the end, a final savoury dish — noodles, rice, ochazuke — is the traditional full stop, called the shime. After that: "O-kaikei o onegaishimasu" — the bill, please.

None of this is law — the otoshi, seat charges and the two-hour seat limits some busy shops set are shop rules, which means shops can also waive them. The actual laws of an izakaya evening are a short list: the drinking age is 20 (it did not move when Japan's age of majority dropped to 18), nobody under 20 may be served, and nobody drives afterwards — in Japan, that prohibition includes bicycles, and it extends to anyone who hands a driver a drink.
