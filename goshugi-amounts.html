Goshugi: How Much Cash to Give at a Japanese Wedding

Amounts from the Zexy Wedding Trend Survey 2024 · Updated November 2026

At a Japanese wedding reception, guests do not bring boxed presents. They bring goshugi — cash, in a ceremonial envelope — and hand it over at the reception desk, never to the couple themselves. Nothing about this is law; it is a dense, widely shared set of customs that every Japanese guest absorbed growing up and nobody explains to the foreign guest. Start with the amount.

Why ¥30,000 is the benchmark

For a friend's wedding, the standard gift is ¥30,000. That figure is not arbitrary. According to the Zexy Wedding Trend Survey 2024 (Recruit Bridal Research Institute, national estimates), hosting one guest costs the couple on average about ¥21,400 in food and drink plus about ¥6,300 in return gifts — roughly ¥27,000. The customary gift rounds that up to a clean ¥30,000 with congratulation on top. You are, by design, slightly more than paying for yourself. That is why the amount moves with your relationship to the couple rather than with your generosity — and why giving far above the range can actually burden a couple, who will feel obliged to return the favour.

These are national figures from one survey series; regional and family customs vary, and in a few regions — parts of Hokkaido are the standard example — fee-based weddings replace the gift system altogether. Closeness also matters more than the category: a best friend often gives ¥50,000.

The number rules — they can move your amount

  • Avoid amounts that divide evenly. An even amount is traditionally read as "can be split in two" — an image of the couple parting. This is why a couple attending together gives ¥50,000 rather than ¥60,000, in one shared envelope.
  • Two figures are avoided outright: ¥40,000 (4 is read as shi, death) and ¥90,000 (9 is read as ku, suffering). If your calculation lands on one, move to the next customary step.
  • The modern exception: ¥20,000. Zexy's published guidance accepts ¥20,000 from students and young guests with low incomes. The usual refinement is in the bills: one ¥10,000 bill and two ¥5,000 bills — three bills, an odd count. For an established adult, ¥30,000 remains the expected figure.
  • ¥80,000 is the lucky even number. Eight widens toward the bottom — suehirogari, ever-widening fortune — so ¥80,000 is welcomed despite being even.
  • Count the bills, too. Odd counts where practical: ¥30,000 is normally three ¥10,000 bills; ¥50,000 is five.

The bills: brand-new, facing front

Wedding money is given in brand-new bills. As the envelope maker Maruai explains it, new bills show you prepared for this day in advance; worn, used bills are the custom for funerals — the opposite occasion. Get them a few days ahead at a bank counter, not on the morning of the wedding. The bills go into the inner envelope with the portrait facing the front of the envelope, portrait at the top, all aligned the same way.

The envelope basics

The envelope (shugi-bukuro) is graded, and it is part of the message. Three checks:

  • The knot. A wedding envelope's cord (mizuhiki) is tied in a musubikiri — a knot that cannot be untied and retied, because this joy should happen once, never to be repeated. The bow knot (cho-musubi) is for joys that may recur, like births — and is wrong for a wedding. If the envelope on the rack has a bow, put it back.
  • The cords. For weddings, ten strands — five doubled, doubly auspicious — in red-and-white or gold-and-silver.
  • The grade matches the amount. Department-store guidance (Takashimaya) gives the working rule: a comparatively simple envelope for ¥10,000–¥30,000; a formal envelope with gold-and-silver cords for ¥50,000 and above. Most envelopes are sold with the amount range printed on the package — use it.

On the front, above the knot, goes the heading — the single character 寿 (kotobuki) is safe everywhere — and below the knot, your full name. The inner envelope carries the amount in formal kanji numerals on the front and your address and name on the back. Carry the finished envelope wrapped in a fukusa cloth, never loose in a bag, and hand it over at the reception desk with both hands and a word of congratulation.

Everything here is custom, not law — and customs differ by region, generation and family. When in doubt, the couple, or a Japanese friend who knows them, outranks any guide.
